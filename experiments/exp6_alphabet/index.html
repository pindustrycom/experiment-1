Exp6: an alphabet, numbers and words, learned from images

The same machinery as exp5 (retina, V1, resonators, somatic cells, structures) in a world where the sets are clean. Letters and numbers come first, then words read letter by letter, then words that name things.

Resonance index, resonance-based semantics and the rule set: Joaquin Philco.

Answer so far

Words can name things without a stored label. A colour patch evokes the word structure that was resonating when the patch was shown with the image of its word: 85% correct on new shades and shapes (chance 25%; 2% with shuffled pairs). The image of a word in a font never seen picks the right patch among 4 in 90% of trials. Words are read at 86% on unseen fonts once the axon also reads resonator phase. Without phase, "no" is read as "on" 26 times in 30.

How each stage works

A

Letters and numbers, with their sets

54% on unseen fonts

Capital letters A to Z and digits 0 to 9, rendered at 28 px in a random ink colour, size, slant and offset. 7 font families are used for training (80 images per character) and 3 families are never seen (Inter, Liberation Serif, FreeMono). The oracle teaches each character and its set, "letter" or "number". Each level grows its own structures with exp4's rule (co-resonance above background, discrimination in sleep).

Unseen fonts, 1,440 imagesCharacter (36)Letter / number

The set "letter" has no shape of its own: averaged over 26 shapes, its structure is no better than chance. A set read through its members works much better. Pairs that cross the sets stay hard on their own: O and 0, I and 1, Z and 2. In many fonts they are nearly the same shape, and people tell them apart by context, which is what words give.

B

Words, read letter by letter

86% on unseen fonts

A word is rendered in one font and ink, one character per cell. The eye fixates each cell for 3 steps, then makes a 1-step saccade. At each step the letter structures from stage A resonate on what the eye sees, and their resonance is the input current of 36 letter cells. Each soma integrates with a leak, and its axon drives resonators at 4 frequencies. Word structures form over those axon nodes. The oracle teaches the word and its set: logic (yes, no, maybe), colour (red, green, blue, yellow) and other (on, bed, bay, sea), which holds anagrams and near neighbours on purpose.

Resonator energy is almost blind to order: N then O rings the same as O then N. The phase of each resonator at the end of the word says when each letter came, and adding it (as ON and OFF cells) removes the order errors. Letters that stage A reads at 54% still give words at 86%, because a word has several letters.

C

Words name colour patches

85% naming, 90% comprehension

400 colour patches (red, green, blue, yellow; random shade, size and position) are each shown together with the image of their word, never a string. The word structure that resonates on the word image gates Hebbian learning on the patch nodes, so the colour's structure is indexed by a word structure. Cyan and magenta patches are never paired with a word.

Naming: a patch evokes the word structure whose association resonates most (chance 25% among the 4 colour words). Comprehension: the image of a word in an unseen font evokes its word structure, which picks the matching patch among 4. With energy-only words and suppression outside the words that were used for naming (learned from the pairing), naming reaches 100% and comprehension 71.5%.

Not working yet: asking about a colour with no word. Only 20% of cyan and magenta patches fall below vigilance. Instead, cyan evokes "green" (96%) and magenta evokes "red" (44%), the nearest named hue. That is a reasonable guess, but under rule 1a the agent should ask.

Next

  • Make vigilance work for naming, so an unnamed colour leads to a question and a new word, as in the purple test of earlier experiments.
  • Give characters context: read O and 0 from the word they sit in (letters around them) instead of in isolation.
  • Use the logic words: answer "yes", "no" or "maybe" as word images to questions such as "is this red?", with "maybe" when two structures resonate equally (a bridge).
